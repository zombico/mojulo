### Exhibits: interactive pieces for a museum, a classroom or an encyclopedia

Built on this branch: one door for exhibits, the tour, rooms with a kiosk, the expert's brief, prepared states,
narration, and two exhibits to start from.

- **One door for exhibits, arranged in halls.** The science, space, earth, life and mathematics study objects, the
  science demos built from laws and the historic cities at their period are all asked for in one place, by hall:
  `science/fission`, `earth/hydro`, `life/dna`, `math/derivative`, `history/pompeii`. A hall lists what it holds, an
  exhibit's card is its manual (a historic city's entry still says what is known, reconstructed or conjectured), and a
  made exhibit's measurements are read back in real units from the same place. The separate tools for making a study
  object and for measuring one give way to it, so the list of tools your agent reads gets shorter; their old names
  still work for a release. Kept setups in your cookbook are recalled by their hall too. Everything already made looks
  exactly as it did. The manuals for building a world (its bases, stage kits and looks) stay where they were.
- **A tour you step through.** An exhibit, or any world, can carry stops. At each one the camera glides to a view, a
  caption explains, and the part being talked about is lit while the rest dims; the stop can say how sure it is
  (known, reconstructed or conjectured for history; simulated, depicted or explained for science) and where its claim
  comes from. A panel along the bottom has the way back and on and a dot for each stop; the arrow keys and a swipe step
  it too, and a link can open on any stop. A toured page looks like an exhibit rather than a workbench: it fills the
  window, a phone's or a kiosk's, its title stands quietly in the corner, the camera and wireframe buttons step aside,
  and on a plain background the subject glows softly in the colour of the part each stop is about. Give the stops when you ask for the exhibit, or add them to one you have; a stop that names a
  camera or a part the exhibit does not have is refused, with the ones it does. A historic town can name its parts
  when asked, each monument and each kind of building (its theatre, its houses, its walls, its fountains), so a stop
  lights the one its caption is about; asked for nothing, it is drawn exactly as before. On a town you can walk
  through, the panel's arrow keys and its camera moves work as they do anywhere else (they were held back until now),
  and a science exhibit's own readout moves to the top so the panel never covers it.
- **Rooms and a kiosk.** Several exhibits join into one gallery, with no score to win and no way to fail: a lobby
  with a card for each room (its hall, its title, how it opens, how many stops), and each room its exhibit, with the
  way back and on to the next. A gallery remembers its rooms, not copies of them, so improving an exhibit improves
  every gallery it stands in. On a kiosk, a single exhibit or a whole gallery plays itself when nobody is touching it,
  each stop held as long as its words take to read, a gallery walking from room to room, and it hands itself to the
  next visitor the moment they touch the screen, in large type with nothing to select by accident. A gallery saves as
  a folder that opens with no internet and no server, the form a kiosk wants.
- **A check made for exhibits.** An exhibit has nothing to win, so it is checked the way a label wall is: does it tell
  a story, does each stop say how sure it is, does a claim taken from the historical record say where it comes from
  (a warning when it does not), can each caption be read in the time a visitor gives it, and does every stop show its
  subject on the screen. It advises and never refuses; whether the exhibit looks right is yours to judge, and whether
  a claim is true is the expert's.
- **The expert's brief.** A curator or teacher says what a visitor should leave understanding, whom it is told for, and
  each fact the exhibit states, with its sources and how sure it is. A stop can say one of those facts and take its
  sources from it, and can tell its caption three ways: for everyone, for young visitors, and in depth. The visitor
  picks with a switch on the panel, a gallery remembers the choice from room to room, and a kiosk's next visitor starts
  with everyone's words. When the expert signs off, the sign-off keeps a copy of every fact as they read it; if a fact
  is changed afterwards, the exhibit says so and asks for the expert again. Nobody but the expert signs, and the
  studio never says the facts are true. Every exhibit has a printable catalogue: what it sets out to teach, each stop
  in every audience's words, each fact with its sources and whether the expert has signed it.
- **Prepared states.** Show one exhibit at several settings and let visitors switch between them: a dam at three
  water levels, a cell before and after it divides, a city in two different years. A toggle, a slider or a carousel
  picks the version, a caption says what is different about it, and the view stays exactly where the visitor turned
  it, so the difference is all that changes. The arrow keys work too, and on a kiosk it steps through the versions by
  itself. Each version is made ahead of time, so it looks the same every time; it saves as a folder that opens offline,
  and it can be a room in a gallery.
- **Narration.** Each stop can be read aloud: give it a recording of its caption, made by the optional local voice, by
  your assistant if it can speak, or by a person, and the exhibit keeps it, so it plays on any machine with no voice
  installed. Visitors turn the sound on with a button (a browser plays nothing until someone touches the page); each
  stop speaks in the words they chose, for everyone, for young visitors or in depth, and when the exhibit plays itself
  on a kiosk it waits for the voice to finish. The caption always shows, so nobody needs the sound, and a stop without
  a recording is simply quiet. The check notices a quiet stop among spoken ones, and a recording that seems to belong
  to another stop.
- **Two exhibits to start.** Pompeii in 79 CE: the town before the eruption, its people in that year's dress, a walk
  of nine stops from its walls and forum to its theatre, houses, water and painted election notices, told for
  everyone and for young visitors, every fact taken from the town's own cited record (and graded by how far that
  record checks it), with the expert's sign-off left open. And hydroelectric power, from water to wire: five toured
  rooms (the dam, the falling water, the turbine, the generator, the whole plant) and a room where the visitor changes
  the water's height, every number quoted being the one the exhibit computes. Ask your agent for either, or for one
  made the same way from your own notes and sources; the orientation gallery tours the first.
