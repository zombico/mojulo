### The lorecube: from a setting's lore to a power system the game can play

Planned on this branch. The first piece is built (the table of what opposes what); the lines after it are still
plans, and each is rewritten as it lands.

- **One table of what opposes what.** The studio already knew many element words, in several separate lists: the
  ones a character is coloured by, the ones that shape a hero's air moves, the ones a party's spells and a monster's
  weaknesses are written in, the ones a beast is grown by. None of them said how two elements stand to each other.
  Now one table does, over those lists and not beside them: two poles (light and dark), opposites in pairs (fire and
  water, wind and earth), a ring where each beats the next (the five phases; fire, ice and lightning), and a wheel
  where each is allied to its neighbours and opposed to the two across. You can name one or write your own, and it is
  checked so every element has a weakness and none stands over the rest. It also gives a monster of any element the
  weaknesses and resistances a party game reads. Nothing in a game uses it yet.
- **One description of a reserve of power, and which of the game's gauges can carry it.** A game already has several
  separate meters, each built for one job: the points special moves draw on, the meter air moves fly on, the stamina
  for sprinting and dodging, the stamina for guarding, the resource a hero's powers spend, and a party member's magic
  points. A setting usually has one idea ("an inner energy that drains as it is used"). You say its name and what
  empties it; later passes add how it comes back, what happens when it runs out, and its numbers. It then tells you
  which meter can carry it, what that meter cannot do, when one idea would end up as two separate bars on screen, and
  what nothing can do yet: pouring the reserve into an ordinary attack is named as a gap, with the smallest change
  that would close it. Nothing in a game uses it yet.
- **Who holds a share of the power, and what can be theirs alone.** A setting gives powers to sides: four factions, a
  light side and a dark side. A game has four kinds of thing a share can belong to: the hero, a class in a party, a
  monster, and a faction of the world. Each can have some things to itself (its element, its powers, its look), shares
  others with the whole game (there is one set of combos, one feel, one menu style per game), and cannot have the
  rest at all. This is now written down as one table, so when a setting asks two sides to differ by something a game
  has only one of, you are told before anything is built. It also says when a side's weakness has nowhere to land (a
  party member has no weaknesses today; a monster in a party game does) and when a side's reserve has no meter of its
  own. A faction, as things stand, is only how its things look. Nothing in a game uses the table yet.
- **Pictures of these relationships, drawn from the same data.** Each arrangement of elements and each reserve can be
  drawn as a diagram: the elements round a circle in their own colours with who beats, opposes and is allied to whom,
  and a reserve with what empties it and the meters that carry it. The drawings are made from the data and checked,
  so they cannot drift from it. A set is kept under `docs/lorecube/`.
- **Made in passes, so the first one stays small.** A piece of lore is made in two kinds of step. The first only
  commits to what the thing is (which elements, in what arrangement) and stops there, however much is asked. Every
  step after that deepens what exists (the reasons, later the numbers and the moves) and may not change what was
  already settled. So a first sketch is quick and stays yours, depth is added as many times as you want it, and a step
  that tries to do everything at once is told so.

- **Describe how power works in your world and get a system a game can play.** A magic system for four factions built
  on the elements; one energy with a light side and a dark side; a reserve that drains as it is used and can be poured
  into an ordinary attack; a faction's technology; a book of monsters. You say it roughly, in detail, or not at all,
  and the studio answers with a system that holds together.
- **Your idea stays yours.** Said roughly, the gaps are filled in. Said in detail, it is followed as written, and
  anything that cannot be built yet is named instead of being dropped.
- **You say what should be true, not how.** "The dark side hits harder and costs you something each time." The game is
  built to that, and you are told, outcome by outcome, whether it was delivered.
- **It starts where the story starts.** The system is first sketched when the game's premise is decided, in your
  words, and filled in when the game is made: the moves, their timing and cost, how each side looks and sounds, and
  how its menus are styled.
- **Sides that are different on purpose.** Factions, colours or poles are given their own share of what a game can do,
  so each plays differently, each has a weakness and none is simply best.
