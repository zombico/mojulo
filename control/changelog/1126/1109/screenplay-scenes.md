### Cutscenes in a game written as a screenplay, shot by the director's rules

- **Write a cutscene the way a script is written.** A scene in a level can now be written as a short screenplay: who
  speaks and what, a feeling in brackets, who crosses to which place, who turns to whom, a pause. The studio's
  cinematics side then stages it on the level itself. It works out where everyone stands and whom they face, and
  shoots it the way a director would: a wide shot to open, over the shoulder for the conversation, a close-up on a
  line said with feeling, the wide again when someone moves, and the camera always on one side of the conversation so
  left stays left. The writer writes only the words; the shots are chosen for them.
- **The scene sets itself up.** Before the first line, the hero walks up to the person it is meeting and everyone turns
  to face as the scene begins, so the shots frame what they were planned to. When the scene ends, the hero is the
  player's again and the camera is its own.
- **Everything a scene could already do still works.** A written scene can be skipped, can carry a choice between two
  stretches of script, can set and read story flags and quests, and can be played by the game's flow as an opening, a
  prologue or an ending. A script that names someone or somewhere the level does not have is caught when the game is
  built and reported by name, while the rest of the game builds.
- **A blocked walk no longer stalls a scene.** A scene that waits for someone to walk somewhere now goes on if the walk
  is stopped by a wall, instead of waiting for ever.
- **A written scene keeps its own timing while the player reads.** Each line of a script now carries how long it
  takes to say, and the scene keeps that time while the line is on screen. A press shows the whole line at once while
  the moment plays on; a line left alone plays out and then waits, the cast listening, for the press. However quickly
  or slowly the player reads, the next line starts on its own beat, so the acting that follows in a later step stays
  in step with the words.
- **Characters act their written scenes.** In a scene written as a script, the characters now perform it in the
  level: their bodies move with the lines, gestures land on the stressed words, and the listener nods and stays alive
  while the other speaks. A realistic character's face plays too: the eyes and brows lead the words, and the eyes find
  whoever they are talking to. When a line has played out and waits for the player, or once the scene is over, everyone
  goes back to standing and breathing as normal. Characters whose faces the game doesn't animate yet still act with
  their bodies.
- **Slow motion and freeze-frames in a written scene.** A script's "Slow motion." now slows the world in the level,
  people walking and everything moving, until "Real time." brings it back; "Freeze." holds everything still for a
  moment. The camera, the text box and the characters' speech stay at normal speed, so the action slows and the words
  never drag. Skipping a scene in slow motion puts the world straight back to normal speed. A hand-written scene can
  call on a game's own slow-motion moments the same way.
- **Say how each character acts.** Each character, the hero included, can be given an acting style, a role (such as
  someone who holds their head high, or one who keeps glancing away) and a way of speaking, and their written scenes
  are played that way: the timing of the lines, the gestures and the faces follow it.
- **The director's notes.** When a game is built, each written scene gets the notes a director would give after a
  read-through: a shot held too long, a speaker turned away from whom they're talking to, a long talk with no
  close-up, a line slowed by slow motion. Each note comes with a suggested fix to the script, and none of them stops
  the build. They appear in the game's design readout and its book. The game's flow storyboard also marks which
  cutscenes are written scenes.
