### A lived-in city district

- **Back yards behind every row of houses.** In a city district you play or look around, the wide empty lawn behind
  each row of houses is now a back yard for every house: a patio or a deck off its back door, a vegetable garden, a
  play set or a trampoline, a shed, a shade tree, chosen so no two neighbours have the same yard. Fences of board,
  lattice, slats, chain-link, pickets, brick, stone or hedge run between the yards and along the back where two rows
  meet. On a hillside a yard that crosses a terrace's step is split into two, with a fence along the top of the wall.
- **Service yards behind shops, a car park beside a low-rise.** The land behind a row of shops is a paved yard with
  bins by each back door and a car now and then; a low-rise apartment block has its residents' car park.
- **Little parks are parks.** A block given over to a park now has two paths across it, a playground, a pavilion,
  benches along the paths and trees on its lawn, as a town's park does.
- **Vacant lots and fields.** A fenced vacant lot has a few young trees that seeded themselves near its fence, and a
  school's field has a pitch with its goals where it is big enough for one.
- **Grass everywhere it belongs.** In the most detailed look, vacant lots, school fields and the courtyards of
  apartment blocks grow the same grass as the lawns round them instead of showing as a flat green colour. No grass
  grows up through a patio, a deck, a shed or a path.
- **Lawns that look kept.** A town's or a city's lawns in the most detailed look are bare in only a few worn places,
  and those places are irregular. They used to run in long straight stripes north to south across every lawn and park,
  which read as a pattern rather than as wear. Islands and trails are drawn as they were.
- **Houses are house-sized.** A pair of semi-detached houses along a main road stood as tall as the five- and
  six-storey buildings beside it. A house is now three storeys at most.
- **Street paint lies on the street.** Lane lines, centre lines, crosswalk bars, streetcar rails, parking-space lines
  and tree pits were placed by the lie of the land, but the road and pavement are drawn in flat panels, so on a hill
  a piece could float up to half a metre over the road or sink into it. Every piece of paint now sits on the road
  that is actually drawn under it, following its slope from end to end, a centimetre over it (it used to be five, enough
  to cast its own shadow). Lamps, signals, poles, hydrants, bins, benches, bike stands and street trees stand on the
  pavement drawn under them in the same way.
- **Site fences follow the ground.** The temporary fences and hoardings round a building site or a vacant lot stood at
  one height for the whole block, so on a terraced block some panels hung in the air and others were buried. Each
  panel now stands on the terrace under it.
- **Smaller, faster-loading city pages.** In the most detailed look a city district's page is about a third smaller
  (158 MB to 101 MB for a played district). Buildings and grounds that are finished in the same material now share
  one texture rather than each carrying its own copy, and the large maps behind the land (where grass grows, the
  ground's shape) are compressed in the page and unpacked by the browser as it loads. Smaller lands are unchanged.
