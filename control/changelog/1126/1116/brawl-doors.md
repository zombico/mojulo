### Beat-em-ups and run & guns: two new games you can ask for

- **Ask for a beat-em-up and you get one to play.** A street seen from the side, and wave after wave of its crowd
  coming at your hero, fought hand to hand: you line up across the street to land a blow. One to four players on one
  screen. Clear the last wave and the level is won; fall too many times and it is lost. You can say how many
  players, how many falls are allowed, which waves come (who is in each and how they arrive), the look, and whether
  your heroes carry their own weapons.
- **Ask for a run & gun and you get the same street fought with guns.** It is the same kind of game with a different
  way of hitting: rounds that fly and can be stepped off or rolled through, grenades, and a crowd that shoots back
  after a warning. You can say which guns your heroes carry.
- **Your heroes are characters, made for the game.** Each player's hero is rolled and made as a character, the way
  a fighting game's fighters are, and drawn in its own body on the street. You can describe them, or bring ones you
  have already made.
- **The crowd is your game's own cast.** Say who the enemies of your game are (a street gang, goblins, a rival
  school and its boss) and they are who comes down the street. You write each wave by the part someone plays (the
  rank and file, a shooter, a tougher one, the boss) or by name, and each enemy fights the way it is: a big one
  grabs and throws, a quick one cuts and runs, a shooter keeps its distance. Enemies that are people are made as
  characters and drawn in their own bodies. In a run & gun everyone on the street carries a gun.
- **You design the street.** Say how far it runs, where it holds your players and who waits at the end. It opens
  on a quiet stretch where nothing comes, so the controls can be learned. Then come its stops: at each one the view
  holds until everyone who came there has fallen, then the street opens again. At the end is the boss, with
  whoever stands with it. Beating the last of them wins the level. The same street works for a beat-em-up and a
  run & gun.
- **The studio says what a street is missing, and builds it anyway.** A street with no boss, or a stop that meets
  fewer enemies than the one before it, is pointed out for you to decide on.
- **A street can be a level of the game you are designing.** A game designed page by page (its intent, its
  enemies, its hero) can now say that one of its levels is a street. It is reviewed, built and checked with the
  rest of the game: its enemies are the game's own, made once and used wherever they appear, and the hero is the
  game's hero. A game that has no hero yet is played by a stand-in, and the studio says so.
- **Up to four can play a street.** Say how many play from the start. The seats go to your game's hero and then
  to the companions who join the party, each in their own body, and anyone with a body can drop in on a second
  controller. A seat nobody fills is played by a stand-in, and the studio says how many.
- **A game can have more than one street.** Each is a level; clearing one opens the next, and the game's menus
  (choosing a level, the result, going on to the next) are drawn in the game's own look.
- **The studio warns when the screen is the wrong shape.** A street runs across the screen. A game made for an
  upright phone shows only a sliver of it, and the studio now says so and how to change it.
- **A street shows the game, not the practice room.** While you play a street you see each player's name and
  health, the falls you have left, which stop you are at, the boss's health while it stands, and a banner when a
  stop clears and the street opens again. The practice readouts and the practice settings no longer appear in a
  level.
- **A street is played through before you get it.** When the studio checks your game it now plays each street
  with a simple player that lines up, closes in and attacks (or fires, in a run & gun), and with one that does
  nothing. A street passes when the first can win it and the second cannot. If the simple player loses, the studio
  says where it ran out of falls and what would ease it: more falls, a thinner wave at that stop, or an easier
  boss. It tells you; it does not change your design.
- **The street a game gets by default can be won by one player.** Its boss now fights as a novice. Measured: at the
  usual level one simple player lost at the boss with all three falls gone, while two players won.
- **Ask for one in a sentence.** "A beat em up in a port town" or "a run and gun through a factory" now gives you
  that game: streets for its levels, a hero made for it, a wide screen. Before, the studio answered that these kinds
  of game could not be made yet. A "side-scrolling shooter" is read as a run & gun. A sentence that names a game
  whose feel the studio knows ("a Mega Man style run and gun") still gets that feel, not a street.
- **A first street forgives more.** The street a sentence lays allows five falls. Measured with a hero made from a
  sentence: the simple player lost it at three falls and won it at five.
- **A run & gun now plays like an arcade one.** Soldiers fall to two or three rounds, your hero falls to a
  few hits, the enemy fires sooner and its rounds fly faster, and each stop sends about twice as many, dropping in
  from above and coming out of doors as well as walking in. The street is narrower, so it reads as a side view, and
  you have five falls. This is one word on a street, its tuning, and you can ask for the older, slower fight
  instead. A beat-em-up is unchanged.
- **The tuning is held to what it claims.** The studio's simple player must still win the default street alone,
  while taking hits. Measured: at a weaker hero it lost at the boss with every fall gone, so the hero was set to
  survive one of the boss's blows.
- **Jump over fire, duck under it.** In a run & gun an enemy's round flies at chest height. Jump early enough and
  it passes under you; jump late and you are caught in the air and knocked down. Hold duck (strong and down
  together) and it passes over you, and you can fire back from the crouch, low. Enemies notice: they aim low at a
  ducked player, and the way out of a low round is a jump. Stepping off a round's line still works as before.
- **Ducking is a move, not a hiding place.** Measured on the default street with three kinds of simple player: one
  that only ducks and fires was hit the most, one that jumps incoming rounds was hit the least, and the plain one
  sat between them.
- **A boss can fight in stages.** In a beat-em-up or a run & gun, say how many stages the boss fight has, or
  write each one: the point in its health where the stage begins, how hard it fights from there, what the stage is
  called, and who it calls in to help. As a stage begins the boss cannot be hurt for a moment, a banner names the
  stage, and its bar across the top shows the stages as segments.
- **A game made from a sentence gets a three-stage boss.** It fights harder at each stage and calls two of its
  rank and file in at the second and third. Measured with the simple player: won in a beat-em-up with two of five
  falls, and in a run & gun with three of five.
- **Not yet:** an enemy that is a beast is drawn as a plain jointed figure, and the game says so; only a person's
  body stands on the street today. A game that names no enemies of its own still meets the stock crowd, drawn as
  plain figures on one open screen: that path is kept for testing. The things that lie on a street to pick up or
  break are not laid, and the number of players is set by the game, not chosen on the screen before a level. No person
  has played these: the studio's simple player has, and it does not jump, throw, use a strong blow or a special, so
  it shows a street can be won, not that it is fun.
