### Beat-em-ups and run & guns: two new games you can ask for

- **Ask for a beat-em-up and you get one to play.** A street seen from the side, and wave after wave of its crowd
  coming at your hero, fought hand to hand: you line up across the street to land a blow. One to four players on one
  screen. Clear the last wave and the level is won; fall too many times and it is lost. You can say how many
  players, how many falls are allowed, which waves come (who is in each and how they arrive), the look, and whether
  your heroes carry their own weapons.
- **Ask for a run & gun and you get the same street fought with guns.** It is the same kind of game with a different
  way of hitting: rounds that fly and can be stepped off or rolled through, grenades, and a crowd that shoots back
  after a warning. You can say which guns your heroes carry.
- **Your heroes are characters, made for the game.** Each player's hero is rolled and made as a character, the way
  a fighting game's fighters are, and drawn in its own body on the street. You can describe them, or bring ones you
  have already made.
- **The crowd is your game's own cast.** Say who the enemies of your game are (a street gang, goblins, a rival
  school and its boss) and they are who comes down the street. You write each wave by the part someone plays (the
  rank and file, a shooter, a tougher one, the boss) or by name, and each enemy fights the way it is: a big one
  grabs and throws, a quick one cuts and runs, a shooter keeps its distance. Enemies that are people are made as
  characters and drawn in their own bodies. In a run & gun everyone on the street carries a gun.
- **You design the street.** Say how far it runs, where it holds your players and who waits at the end. It opens
  on a quiet stretch where nothing comes, so the controls can be learned. Then come its stops: at each one the view
  holds until everyone who came there has fallen, then the street opens again. At the end is the boss, with
  whoever stands with it. Beating the last of them wins the level. The same street works for a beat-em-up and a
  run & gun.
- **The studio says what a street is missing, and builds it anyway.** A street with no boss, or a stop that meets
  fewer enemies than the one before it, is pointed out for you to decide on.
- **A street can be a level of the game you are designing.** A game designed page by page (its intent, its
  enemies, its hero) can now say that one of its levels is a street. It is reviewed, built and checked with the
  rest of the game: its enemies are the game's own, made once and used wherever they appear, and the hero is the
  game's hero. A game that has no hero yet is played by a stand-in, and the studio says so.
- **Not yet:** an enemy that is a beast is drawn as a plain jointed figure, and the game says so; only a person's
  body stands on the street today. A game that names no enemies of its own still meets the stock crowd, drawn as
  plain figures on one open screen: that path is kept for testing. A street level is for one player today. The things that lie on a street to pick up or
  break are not laid, a game is one street long, and the readouts on screen are still the practice room's. Nobody
  has played these; the studio has checked that each level loads, that the street holds at a stop and brings its
  enemies in, and that winning and losing end the level.
